Terms for novices

Lock-up. A contractual "you can't sell yet" on pre-IPO shareholders (employees, early investors, the founder), so insider selling can't hit the market all at once on day one. It expires on set dates. Buying on the open market after the debut, you were never locked. Float. The freely tradable shares, as a portion of the whole company. SpaceX has ~13B total shares but only ~5% floated at IPO. The rest sits locked. Small float chasing heavy demand is a big part of why the price spiked. Widen the float and, all else equal, price falls. S-1 / 424(b)(4). The SEC registration filing (S-1) and the final priced prospectus (424(b)(4)) SpaceX filed to go public. Filed under legal liability, so they beat any news summary. Every share count and lock-up term here comes from them. On SEC EDGAR under CIK 1181412.
02

The eligible supply

The full schedule is spelled out in the prospectus, in its Underwriting and Shares Eligible for Future Sale sections.

Right now demand is chasing that ~5% float. That imbalance does much of the work of holding the price up. The lock-ups widen the float on a set schedule. The first release alone is bigger than the entire slice currently trading, which is a big part of why the word "flood" gets used.

The core number. The first release frees ~911.5M shares. At the $135 IPO price that's ~$123B of stock that could hit the market, versus the ~$86B tradable as of mid-July. One release is larger than the whole existing float.

Two things stand between eligible supply and actual selling.

  1. 1It's staggered, unlike the single 180-day cliff most IPOs use. Shares release in stages across months, so the market can price each one as it comes instead of absorbing one shock all at once.
  2. 2The biggest locked holders are the least likely to sell. The founder and the big long-term backers (like Google, in for years as a strategic bet) face no calendar pressure to exit. Employees and older venture funds usually do sell into their windows, so those smaller tranches track pressure more tightly.
Not every unlocked share is equal supply. An unlock date sets how much can trade. Pressure still depends on who holds it. Diversifying employees and older venture stakes usually sell into their windows, so those drips are real supply. August volume is the first read on which mix showed up. The earnings report is just the starting gun written into the lock-up contract, not a sell signal for every holder.

A price trigger adds supply into strength. A bonus batch of ~455.8M shares unlocks only if the stock closes at or above $175.50 on 5 of the 10 trading days ending with the Aug 4 report. So a rally doesn't reward holders for waiting. It releases more supply right where they'd want to sell. The higher it climbs, the more stock the structure lets loose. The trigger sits 30% above the IPO price by construction, and with the stock trading below $135 since mid-July into that final 10-session window, it is effectively dead.

The buyers who had no choice

While locked shares wait to come free, index funds were being forced to buy.

On July 7, 2026, SPCX joined the Nasdaq-100 (the index behind funds like QQQ), twenty-five days after debut, after a rule change let big new listings skip the usual wait. Per Nasdaq, more than 200 products track that index with over $800B globally, and the cash index funds among them have to buy. Cash trackers and leveraged wrappers (QLD, TQQQ) took the Jul 6 close, $160.42, as their entry. J.P. Morgan put the Nasdaq-100 piece near $4.3B (an analyst estimate, not a filing). The wider linked complex runs higher. All of it pointed at the same thin ~5% float.

It did not lift the tape. On inclusion day SPCX fell. The index-inclusion pop itself has faded: the average abnormal return on joining the S&P 500 fell from 7.4% in the 1990s to 0.3% in the past decade (Greenwood and Sammon). "Money that must move" is a price effect that no longer shows up on average. By the verified Jul 21 close of $123.54, that entry was ~23% underwater. Passive funds missed the IPO allocation, then had to buy on a pre-announced date, likely after others had already positioned to sell into that known demand.

What cushions the oversupply

What history says. In the classic study of 1,948 IPO lock-up expiries (Field and Hanka, 2001), unlock days bruised prices, they did not crash them. The average move was a three-day drop of about 1.5%, even though every expiry date was known in advance. The lasting change was volume: a permanent ~40% rise once shares came free. The pressure was concentrated where this page ranks it High: venture-backed firms dropped more, and venture funds sold harder than executives. Base rate only: the sample is 1988–1997 single-cliff lock-ups on far smaller companies, and SPCX's staggered design spreads the effect across many dates.

Ranked by likely impact, highest first.

  1. 1Not every unlocked share is equal supply. Headline numbers count every freed share the same. Early pressure is likelier to be employee and older-venture selling than Alphabet-scale exits, so realized August supply should land below the ~$123B ceiling.
  2. 2The biggest stakes are the least likely to move. Musk, Alphabet, and Fidelity dominate the locked total and have no forced timeline. The likeliest sellers, older venture funds and diversifying employees, hold the smaller slices.
  3. 3Patient sellers pace themselves. A large holder dumping into a thin float wrecks their own exit price, so rational exits usually dribble out over months. Self-interest tends to throttle the flow, unless fund mechanics force a seller's hand.
  4. 4The structure staggers supply by contract. Releases arrive in stages through Dec 8, with one price-conditional piece only, so the market prices each tranche instead of absorbing one shock.
  5. 5Institutional buyers can absorb size, at their price. Fewer, larger buyers sit opposite many small employee sellers and can take blocks off the visible book. Capacity is proven. Willingness depends on where their valuation work clears, which may be below the current print.
  6. 6Passive demand grows with the float. Index funds must buy mechanically, and where index weights track the tradable float, unlocks themselves can raise the weight and force incremental buying at rebalances. July 7 showed inclusion is no guaranteed lift. CoinDesk reported that prior big adds (Palantir, Strategy) peaked around or before their inclusion dates, and whether the Nasdaq-100's method raises SPCX's weight on unlocks is unverified.
  7. 7A conviction tier waits below. Thesis buyers exist: ARK has bought SPCX on the way down and keeps buying dips. That's a sentiment floor and a next-day-verifiable signal (ARK publishes its ETF trades daily), not absorption capacity.
What this page does not cover. This maps supply timing, not price. Between unlock dates the tape often moves on positioning left out here: short interest and the cost to borrow, options open interest and dealer hedging, the float-adjusted index weight, and how deep real demand is. On a thin ~5% float, those forces can dominate for weeks while the supply schedule sits unchanged. Read this as the map of when supply structurally shifts, not a forecast of where the price goes. The unlock dates matter most as the moments that map meets positioning: a big block freeing into a heavily shorted, thin float is when the squeeze-prone regime can break.
